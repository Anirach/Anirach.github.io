## 0. Book extract (verbatim, Chapter 11)

### 0.1 Chapter argument (printed p. 47, English companion)

> The useful unit of workforce redesign is the task and the social choice is how authority opportunity risk and productivity gains are shared.

> A role combines routine processing, prediction, judgment, relationships, physical work, exception handling, and accountability. AI may automate some components, augment others, and increase the importance of the tasks that remain human-owned. Exposure studies identify where change may occur but cannot tell one organization how many jobs will disappear. Capability, workflow design, demand growth, regulation, adoption cost, and management choices shape the result.

> The ILO refined global index estimates one in four workers are in occupations with some generative AI exposure, while 3.3 percent of global employment is in its highest category. Because occupations contain tasks requiring human input, it concludes transformation is more likely than complete automation. Exposure is not observed displacement. MIT Humans in the Loop describes early company experiments aimed at bottlenecks, expert synthesis, and learning curves, with some workers shifting toward supervisory control. These are early patterns, not settled outcomes.

> Begin with task decomposition. For each role identify the task, frequency, time, knowledge, consequence of error, data, and feedback. Classify the future as eliminate, automate, augment, create, or retain under human authority. Reassemble the role so removed routine work is replaced by explicit higher-value responsibility, not simply a larger workload.

> Skills must follow the redesigned workflow. General AI literacy covers capabilities, limitations, data rules, hallucination, security, and escalation. Role-specific proficiency teaches people to verify outputs, frame decisions, supervise tools, handle exceptions, and document accountability. Leaders need enough technical and economic literacy to set risk appetite and investment. Domain experts need evaluation and process-design skills. AI teams need operations, human factors, and change capability. Frontline people need protected time to practice with real work.

> A prompt course alone is not reskilling. Combine short instruction, supervised practice, job-based assignments, coaching, and demonstrated proficiency. Evaluate Thai-language quality and local context directly. An English benchmark is not evidence of equal usefulness. Include worker voice before workflow choices become difficult to reverse. Tell people what changes, what data is collected, how it affects performance decisions, which decisions remain human, and how errors are challenged. Monitor work intensification, autonomy, privacy, psychological safety, and uncompensated review labor.

> Productivity gains are not automatically shared. Leadership should decide how verified time savings are allocated among customer value, quality, learning, reduced workload, growth, and financial return. Without that decision, AI may simply compress schedules and raise targets.

### 0.3 Working session — *Role redesign and workforce compact* (verbatim agenda, printed p. 48)

> Select one role and map its tasks. Score frequency, time, error consequence, data readiness, and human value. Classify tasks as eliminate, automate, augment, create, or human-owned. Assemble the future role and identify workload or accountability gaps. Define skills, practice assignments, and proficiency evidence. Agree data use, notice, appeal, redeployment, and feedback commitments. Name the role owner, worker representative, pilot cohort, and review date.

Thai mirror (verbatim, printed p. 49, under **เวิร์กช็อป Role redesign and workforce compact**):

**Worksheet steps, enumerated for the `[WORKSHEET]` table:**
1. Select one role and map its tasks.
2. Score each task: Frequency · Time · Error consequence · Data readiness · Human value.
3. Classify: eliminate · automate · augment · create · human-owned.
4. Assemble the future role; identify workload or accountability gaps.
5. Define skills, practice assignments, proficiency evidence.
6. Agree commitments: data use · notice · appeal · redeployment · feedback.
7. Name: role owner · worker representative · pilot cohort · review date.

### 0.8 Masterclass §16 — book's own paraphrase (Appendix A, printed p. 64; video 43:18–46:37)

Paraphrase-only. Never quote the presenter. Book's paraphrase (this is the book's text, and may itself be paraphrased further, never presented as the speaker's words):

> The question "Which jobs will disappear?" is too coarse because every job combines tasks: searching, predicting, communicating, judging, creating trust, and accepting responsibility. ## 3. Number cards

### Card 1 — ILO: one in four workers
- **Value / unit:** one in four (≈25%) of workers
- **Denominator / population:** global employment, occupations mapped to ISCO; task-level scoring aggregated to occupations
- **Comparison:** against workers in occupations with *no* measured GenAI exposure; and by income group, 11% of total employment in low-income countries vs 34% in high-income countries
- **Task / setting:** an occupational **exposure index**, not an observation of hiring, firing or output
- **Source:** [30] ILO, 20 May 2025. Verbatim: *"Globally, one in four workers are in an occupation with some GenAI exposure."*
- **Book's Boundary line (must appear):** "exposure is not observed displacement; effects vary by occupation, gender, income level, adoption, and policy."
- **TH sentence:** ดัชนีของ ILO ประเมินว่าคนทำงานหนึ่งในสี่ทั่วโลกอยู่ในอาชีพที่มี Generative AI Exposure อยู่บ้าง<sup>[30]</sup> — ตัวเลขนี้เป็นการวัด "ความสัมผัสเทคโนโลยี" ระดับอาชีพ ไม่ใช่จำนวนตำแหน่งที่หายไปจริง การสัมผัสเทคโนโลยีกับการแทนที่งาน (exposure versus displacement) เป็นคนละเรื่อง และผลจริงยังต่างกันตามอาชีพ เพศ ระดับรายได้ของประเทศ การนำไปใช้ และนโยบาย
- **EN sentence:** The ILO's refined global index estimates that one in four workers worldwide are in an occupation with some generative-AI exposure<sup>[30]</sup> — a measure of where tasks *could* be affected, not a count of jobs lost. Exposure is not observed displacement, and the effect varies by occupation, gender, country income level, adoption and policy.

### Card 2 — ILO: 3.3% in the highest exposure category
- **Value / unit:** 3.3 percent of global employment
- **Denominator / population:** global employment (same index as Card 1)
- **Comparison:** against the ~25% with *some* exposure; and within the 3.3%, female employment 4.7% vs male employment 2.4%
- **Task / setting:** the top band of the same occupational exposure index
